I have sat in offices that smell like a product decision. Citrus in the lobby, something "calm" in the meeting room, nobody asked the person with a migraine. Retail has used scent to hold people in the aisle for years. That is a conversion tactic. It is not a wellness program, and it does not become one because the slide deck said wellbeing.

Digital products cannot smell. Physical rooms can, and they do it without an opt-out more often than I like. Ambient scent in a workplace is an accessibility problem first. Fragrance is a migraine trigger for a lot of people. Asthma, pregnancy, and sensory load (ADHD, autism, a week of too many screens) change how a hallway feels. You cannot ship a "calm" HVAC fragrance and call it inclusive. Consent is the control that is usually missing. A sign that the lobby is scented is the floor, not a favor. A way to work in unscented air is the actual feature.

Sometimes useful is personal and small. I mark my own desk with a short, timed session I can turn off. That is a ritual, not a UX for the floor. The crossover from retail research is easy to abuse: dwell time is not the same job as a builder trying to think. I do not want my home office to convert me. I want to leave the chair.

If you design spaces, treat scent like sound. Default off. Volume low when it is on. A way out. Do not hide a mister in a plant. Do not run oils in a room with a cat because the brand said "natural." Natural is not a safety certificate, and coworkers are not testers. I will take a quiet room with a window over a lobby that smells like a candle shop.

Theater is the LED wall of smell: a signature scent for the company, a quarterly "mood." Useful is the person who asked before they switched the machine on. I write software and I write these notes. Both have users who cannot close a dialog if the dialog is the air.

— JV · Dark Heart Labs.
