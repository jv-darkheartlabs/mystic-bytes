## Hook & thesis
The film opens with the Blitz — an addition, and a good one: these are children already fluent in war before Narnia asks them to fight one. Adamson shoots Lewis's winter beautifully, Swinton plays Jadis as genuine aristocratic evil rather than camp, and Henley's Lucy carries the book's faith at face value, which is the only way it works. Then the third act inflates Lewis's brisk battle into a *Lord of the Rings* set-piece, and Aslan's resurrection — the book's still center — has to compete with griffon cavalry. **Verdict:** Faithful in sequence and sincere in belief, which for Lewis is the fidelity that matters; but the intimate, odd, narrated little book keeps being outspent by the blockbuster built around it.

## The contextual pivot
*The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* opened in December 2005 into a market *The Lord of the Rings* had created and *Harry Potter* was occupying: Disney and Walden Media’s wager that Lewis’s seven slim volumes could anchor a franchise of the same weight. The production carried a double constituency no other fantasy property had — the childhood readership of half the English-speaking world, and the Christian audience for whom Narnia is doctrine in story form, courted deliberately in the marketing. Adamson, an animation director from the *Shrek* films, was the bridge hire: family credentials with effects fluency. The film succeeded on the scale its moment required — a global hit, a launched series — and then the series itself became the cautionary tale, each sequel diminishing until the franchise stalled, leaving this first film as the era’s clearest specimen of the post-*Rings* land rush: a beloved intimate book, faithfully kept and expensively enlarged, at the exact historical moment when enlargement was the only language studios spoke.

## Deep-dive critique
McAlpine shoots Narnia in two weathers: the Witch’s winter as a beautiful, airless stillness — snow that muffles rather than sparkles — and the thaw as the film’s true special effect, color returning to the world in rivers and blossom as Aslan approaches, Lewis’s theology rendered as cinematography. Adamson’s direction is strongest where the book is smallest: the wardrobe crossing held on Lucy’s face, Mr. Tumnus’s tea, the Stone Table sequence played in near-silence, where the film finally trusts stillness and the resurrection stops the spectacle cold. Henley’s Lucy is the production’s foundation — faith at face value, the only register in which Lewis’s argument works — and Keynes gives Edmund’s treachery its proper grubby motives: spite, hunger, a smaller brother’s grievance, so his pardon lands as grace rather than plot. Swinton’s Jadis is the film’s claim to greatness: aristocratic evil, glacial and genuinely frightening, Turkish Delight offered like a treaty. Neeson’s Aslan is warm where Lewis’s lion is ‘not safe’ — a softening the film’s theology can afford less than it thinks. The third act pays the era’s tax: Lewis’s brisk battle inflated into a *Rings*-scale set-piece with griffon cavalry and war-film grammar wrapped around a story whose climax was supposed to be a broken table and an empty tomb. Gregson-Williams’s score swells dutifully over both registers. Faithful in sequence and sincere in belief — which for Lewis is the fidelity that matters — and yet the intimate, odd, narrated little book keeps being outspent by the blockbuster built around it.

## Adaptation ledger
- **Character backstory:** The film condenses the Pevensie siblings' background, focusing on their arrival in Narnia rather than their wartime experiences, which slightly diminishes the depth of their motivations.
- **Aslan's dialogue:** Some of Aslan's philosophical musings are simplified for clarity, losing the layered richness present in Lewis's text.
- **The climax:** The film maintains the central battle scene but adds visual spectacles that diverge from the book's more intimate focus on personal sacrifice and bravery.
- **The Witch's defeat:** The film emphasizes a more action-oriented resolution, contrasting with the book’s thematic exploration of redemption and grace.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument: whether scale is a translation or a takeover. The room divides between those who say 2005 audiences needed the epic frame to take a children's book seriously and those who find Lewis's power precisely in the smallness — a wardrobe, a lamppost, a stone table — that spectacle keeps drowning.

**Questions for the room:**

- Swinton's Witch versus Lewis's: colder, grander — or the same evil with better tailoring?
- The Blitz prologue: earned context, or the film apologizing for its source's whimsy?
- Aslan's dialogue is simplified throughout. What does a plainer Aslan do to the theology?
- Edmund's betrayal is the book's moral engine. Does the film give his treachery — and his pardon — their full weight, or hurry past them to the battle?
- Neeson's Aslan is warm where Lewis's is "not safe." Which lion is actually on screen?
