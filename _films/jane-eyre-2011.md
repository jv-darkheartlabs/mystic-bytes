## Hook & thesis
Fukunaga shoots Brontë the way the moors deserve — natural light, storm shadow, a Thornfield that presses on the walls of every frame. What the film cannot do is wait. **Verdict:** A faithful-looking *Jane Eyre* whose beauty is real and whose haste is fatal to the one thing the novel is about: trust, accrued slowly.

## The contextual pivot
Cary Joji Fukunaga’s *Jane Eyre* arrived in 2011, in the middle of a prestige-literary cycle busy re-costuming the nineteenth century — and it distinguished itself by subtraction: no heritage gloss, no plush interiors, a Thornfield shot by Adriano Goldman in available gloom, weather doing the production design. Fukunaga came to it from *Sin Nombre*, a film about migration and violence in contemporary Central America, which reads in hindsight as preparation: both are stories of a young woman crossing hostile country toward an uncertain household. The adaptation’s boldest structural choice is its frame — the film opens with Jane fleeing across the moor and tells Thornfield in flashback, converting Brontë’s slow chronological accrual into a mystery of how she came to be at the Rivers’ door. Moira Buffini’s script keeps the novel’s spine and several of its sentences. What the film became, in the long lineage of *Jane Eyre*s, is the gothic-realist one: the version that trusts mist, candlelight, and Wasikowska’s stillness to do what earlier versions did with score and speech.

## Deep-dive critique
Goldman’s cinematography is the film’s argument: Thornfield lit by windows and candle flames, faces half-lost in genuine dark, the moors shot in weather rather than postcard light — a gothic of exposure instead of ornament, in which Jane’s plainness reads as the only honest surface in the house. The flashback structure buys its opening mystery at a real price: by starting with the flight, Fukunaga converts the novel’s accrual into suspense, and the childhood material — Lowood, Helen Burns, the red-room — is compressed into strokes, vivid and brief, so the film’s Jane arrives at Thornfield with her formation summarized rather than lived. Wasikowska plays her as watchfulness itself: still, assessing, withholding — a performance of interiority by refusal, which honors the novel’s Jane, whose passion is all banked, and risks, in the quieter stretches, reading as absence. Fassbender’s Rochester is younger and more plausibly dangerous than the book’s, his gruffness eroticized in the modern manner; the chemistry is real, and it does some of the work the missing months of acquaintance should have done. Marianelli’s score — solo violin over piano — supplies the longing the performances decline to state. The flaws are the adaptation’s own arithmetic: Bertha is atmosphere more than person, glimpsed and shrieking; St. John Rivers is reduced to a doorway; and the ending, Jane’s return, lands with a speed that turns the novel’s earned reunion into a beautiful ellipsis. The film trusts images the way the novel trusts sentences, and the exchange rate is visible: everything Brontë builds by duration, Fukunaga must build by intensity — and intensity, unlike duration, cannot prove trust.

## Adaptation ledger
- **Character Development**: The film streamlines secondary characters, such as Helen Burns and Miss Temple, reducing their narrative weight compared to the novel.
- **Pacing of Romance**: The romance between Jane and Rochester is presented with rapid intensity, potentially glossing over the slow build of tension found in the text.
- **Omitted Themes**: The film downplays certain social critiques regarding class and gender, focusing primarily on the emotional core of Jane and Rochester's relationship.
- **Visual Symbolism**: The film translates the novel’s own storm symbolism — the chestnut tree split by lightning on the night of the proposal — into weather-driven cinematography, making literal what Brontë keeps emblematic.
- **Ending**: The film opts for a visually dramatic conclusion that diverges slightly from the novel's more introspective resolution.

## Discussion launchpad
Every adaptation of *Jane Eyre* is a negotiation with time: the novel's power is accrual, and cinema is impatient. Fukunaga negotiates beautifully and loses on tempo. The question for the room is whether that loss is the price of the form or a failure of nerve.

**Questions for the room:**

- Does the film's natural-light gothic deepen Jane, or decorate her?
- The novel's social critique — class, gender, the governess's impossible position — is thinned to backdrop. What does the film lose with it?
- Wasikowska plays Jane as watchfulness itself. Is withholding the same as interiority?
- Where exactly does the ending stop being earned — the return, the reunion, or the speed of both?
- If an adaptation keeps the atmosphere and loses the accrual, what has it actually adapted?
