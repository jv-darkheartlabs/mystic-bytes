## Hook & thesis
Rice's own screenplay keeps the confession frame and the long 18th-century unspooling, and Jordan shoots it as candlelit decadence — Rousselot's golds and rot, Goldenthal's waltzing dread. The casting that scandalized Rice's readers became the film's argument: Cruise plays Lestat as appetite with perfect manners, Pitt plays Louis as a conscience that won't die, and eleven-year-old Dunst plays Claudia as the story's true tragedy — an adult mind sentenced to a doll's body forever. **Verdict:** Faithful, lush, and quietly censored at its center: the novel's erotic charge between its vampires is diffused into atmosphere and subtext for a 1994 multiplex, so the film aches beautifully about everything except what the book was actually about.

## The contextual pivot
*Interview with the Vampire* opened in November 1994 after eighteen years in development — Rice’s 1976 novel had been optioned almost immediately and passed through studios, scripts, and rumored castings for a generation — and it arrived carrying the decade’s loudest casting scandal: Tom Cruise as Lestat, a choice Rice publicly denounced before the film opened and publicly recanted after seeing it. David Geffen’s production was a prestige horror bet at blockbuster scale, Neil Jordan directing off *The Crying Game*’s heat, and the film landed in a culture mid-reckoning about desire and death — the AIDS era’s shadow lies across its story of beautiful men, blood exchange, and wasting in a way the novel, written before, could only prefigure. It was a hit; it made Rice’s vampires the decade’s defining ones; and its compromises are as historical as its successes: the novel’s erotics diffused into longing for a multiplex audience, immortality’s price kept, its pleasures politely fogged.

## Deep-dive critique
Rousselot shoots the film as candlelit rot — gold leaf and mildew, New Orleans splendor decaying into Parisian catacombs — and Jordan directs the centuries as a single long evening, the editing letting decades elapse in dissolves so Louis’s narration feels like what it is: a man for whom time has stopped meaning sequence. Cruise’s Lestat is the film’s vindicated gamble: appetite with perfect manners, charm played as predation — the only one on screen enjoying eternity, which makes him its most honest character and its monster in the same gesture. Pitt’s Louis is the necessary counterweight and the film’s drag: conscience performed as passivity, beautiful and inert, his guilt curdling from tragedy into habit across two hours. The film belongs, whenever she’s on screen, to Dunst — eleven years old, playing an adult mind sentenced to a doll’s body, her Claudia the one performance that finds the story’s actual horror: not death but arrest, growth forbidden forever. Goldenthal’s score waltzes the dread, period instruments curdling into dissonance. The costs: the Théâtre des Vampires sequence, the book’s set-piece of cruelty as art, is compressed to a flourish; Armand’s menace is softened into Banderas’s melancholy beauty; and the central relationship’s erotic charge — the novel’s engine — is rendered as atmosphere, glances, and one famous wrist. What survives the fogging is the novel’s grief: a film about the price of forever that aches, beautifully, in every direction except the one its source pointed.

## Adaptation ledger
- Retained the core narrative of Louis's existential crisis and Lestat's manipulative nature.
- Claudia's fate is kept from the book: executed by sunlight in the well, with Louis's revenge following — the adaptation's most faithful cruelty.
- Expanded the setting and time period visually to enhance the atmospheric quality.
- Introduced additional scenes to flesh out the relationship dynamics, particularly between Louis and Lestat.
- Maintained the first-person perspective, but adapted it to a more cinematic storytelling approach.
