## Hook & thesis
Goldsman's script transcribes the novel's relay race almost clue for clue — Louvre, cryptex, Teabing's lecture, Rosslyn — and Howard shoots the exposition like chase scenes because exposition is all there is. Hanks plays Langdon as a man thinking at gunpoint; Tautou's Sophie is thinned from the book's cryptographer to a lineage with a face; McKellen, alone, performs as if the material were Shakespeare and nearly makes it so. **Verdict:** Perfectly faithful and perfectly illustrative of the source's ceiling: a thriller whose ideas arrive as dialogue and leave as trivia, ending in a revelation the film can only kneel in front of because it never built the faith to argue with.

## The contextual pivot
*The Da Vinci Code* opened the Cannes festival in May 2006 to the decade’s most public critical drubbing — and then made $760 million anyway, which is the film’s whole historical meaning in one sentence. Brown’s novel had been a publishing earthquake, the kind of book sold in airports to people who did not buy books, and its adaptation was less a film production than a custody transfer: Howard and Goldsman, the *A Beautiful Mind* team, hired as respectable custodians; the Louvre granting filming permission as a national event; the Church conducting a parallel publicity campaign of objection that functioned, as such campaigns do, as marketing. The film arrived pre-argued — boycotts, op-eds, a cottage industry of rebuttal books — so that its actual content barely mattered to its reception. What it proved, permanently, was the existence of a global audience for the lecture-thriller: exposition as spectacle, heresy as tourism, and a revelation delivered at a tomb in Scotland to a heroine who turns out to be the plot’s answer rather than its agent.

## Deep-dive critique
Totino shoots Paris and London in handsome thriller murk, Howard directing with his trademark competence — everything legible, nothing lingering — and the film’s central problem is visible in its best technique: the flashback visualizations, Langdon’s lectures rendered as ghostly overlays on the past, are the movie inventing a grammar for exposition because exposition is all the source provides. Hanks plays Langdon as decency under pressure, a professor thinking at gunpoint, and brings more interiority than the script stocks; Tautou, the book’s cryptographer, is thinned to a lineage with a face, her expertise reassigned to the men around her — the adaptation’s most consequential cut, made quietly. McKellen’s Teabing is the film’s one great performance, a lecture delivered as seduction by an actor performing as if the material were Shakespeare, and his villa sequence is the only stretch where the ideas feel dangerous instead of laminated. Bettany’s Silas is physical commitment in search of a character. Zimmer’s score does the awe-work the arguments can’t, strings swelling over Rosslyn as if underscoring a faith the film never built. The costs are the source’s ceiling, faithfully reproduced: clues that arrive on schedule stop being suspense; the revelation, when it comes, can only be knelt in front of; and 149 minutes of relay produce the strange sensation of a film that never stops moving and never gets anywhere. Perfectly faithful, perfectly illustrative — of the book, and of fidelity’s limits when the book is a machine.

## Adaptation ledger
- **Character Depth:** The film simplifies some characters' backgrounds, particularly Sophie Neveu, who in the book has a more nuanced history that informs her motivations.
- **Symbolic Layers:** The intricate analysis of historical symbols and religious iconography is glossed over in favor of action-driven sequences.
- **Ending Revelation:** The film retains the major plot twist but minimizes the philosophical discussions that accompany it in the novel.
- **Omissions:** Several characters and subplots that add depth to the narrative are omitted, such as the nuance of Langdon's internal conflict regarding faith.
- **Dialogue Alterations:** Key dialogues that explore the implications of faith versus reason are largely condensed or removed, impacting thematic richness.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument: what an adaptation owes a book whose effects are all mechanical. The room divides between those who call this the model of the faithful transcription and those who say fidelity to a puzzle is the easiest kind — the film never has to decide what the book *means*, because the book outsourced meaning to its twist.
