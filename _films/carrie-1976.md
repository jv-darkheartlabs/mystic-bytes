## Hook & thesis
King built *Carrie* out of documents — fake journalism, testimony, a survivor's memoir — a tragedy already being investigated. De Palma throws the collage away and shoots it straight: a girl, a fanatic mother, a school full of casual cruelty, and one long fuse. Spacek plays Carrie as feral and hopeful in the same breath; Laurie's Margaret is the film's true gothic engine, scripture as a locked door. **Verdict:** Faithful in event, transformed in meaning — the novel mourns a town; the film indicts a gym full of laughing faces and lets the audience sit among them, which is why the prom still lands like a crime the viewer helped commit.

## The contextual pivot
*Carrie* was the first Stephen King adaptation and the film that made both their careers in cinema: King was a schoolteacher with one published novel, De Palma a Hitchcock disciple with art-house thrillers behind him, and the property was cheap — which bought De Palma freedom. Released in November 1976, into the decade of the American horror renaissance — *The Exorcist* and *The Texas Chain Saw Massacre* just behind it, *Halloween* just ahead — it stood apart from all of them by being a tragedy first: a high-school film whose monster is the school’s cruelty and the home’s religion, and whose supernatural payload detonates only in the last fifteen minutes. Spacek and Laurie were both Oscar-nominated, a rarity for horror then and now, and the prom sequence entered the permanent grammar of the genre — imitated, parodied, and quoted until the image of the girl in the blood needs no caption. Every King adaptation since, four decades of them, has been measured against the first one’s economy: ninety-eight minutes, no mythology, all fuse.

## Deep-dive critique
Tosi’s cinematography — he replaced Mankofsky early in the shoot — gives De Palma a softer, more romantic image than the director’s thrillers usually wear: diffusion and slow zooms for Carrie’s private world, the shower scene’s steamy, terrible slowness, then the prom in saturated ballroom color that turns, in the famous split-screen passage, into a diptych of a girl’s face and a town’s destruction. De Palma’s editing is the film’s clock: the prom sequence cross-cuts the bucket, the ballots, the conspirators, and Tommy’s smile with a patience that makes the audience complicit — you are given every fact in time to dread the sum. Spacek, twenty-six playing seventeen, does the film’s essential work: her Carrie is not a monster in waiting but a girl visibly rehearsing happiness — the mirror practice, the dress, the walk into the gym — so the massacre reads as grief with powers. Laurie’s Margaret, all whispered scripture and sudden violence, is gothic in the true sense, a faith rotted into a locked room, and she plays her without a single wink, which is why she frightens. Donaggio’s score alternates lullaby strings with stabbing dissonance, a mother’s-love theme curdling. The costs are the era’s and the director’s: the shower scene’s camera lingers past empathy into the leering the film elsewhere condemns, and King’s documentary frame — the town’s collective guilt, investigated — is gone entirely, narrowing the indictment to a gym, a mother, and a bucket. The invented final gag, the hand from the grave, cheapens and seals it at once: Carrie’s story ends the way her life was lived, with one more ambush.

## Adaptation ledger
- Carrie's telekinetic powers are visually represented through innovative special effects, enhancing the emotional weight of her struggle.  
- The portrayal of Carrie's mother, Margaret White, is intensified in the film, highlighting her oppressive nature more than in the novel.  
- The prom scene differs slightly in its execution, focusing more on the catastrophic aftermath than the initial buildup.  
- Key dialogues and scenes from the book are retained, ensuring the film's fidelity to the source material while adding cinematic flourishes.  
- The ending is altered just enough to amplify the horror, creating an unforgettable finale that diverges subtly from King's original conclusion.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument: who is the prom for? The room divides on whether De Palma's split-screen apocalypse is Carrie's revenge, the audience's punishment, or a spectacle that secretly sides with the cruelty it condemns — the shower scene's slow, leering camera has been part of that argument since 1976.

**Questions for the room:**

- The opening shower scene — empathy's foundation, or exploitation the film never answers for?
- Laurie's Margaret is intensified past King's. Does the film need a monster at home to excuse the ones at school?
- The novel's documentary frame spreads blame across a whole town and its institutions. What does losing it do to the moral accounting?
- Sue Snell's guilt and Tommy's kindness survive the adaptation. Do they complicate the revenge, or cushion it?
- The final hand-from-the-grave gag was invented for the film. Cheap shock — or the correct refusal to let Carrie rest?
