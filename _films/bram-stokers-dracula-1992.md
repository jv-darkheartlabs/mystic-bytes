## Hook & thesis
Everything here is excess, deliberately: Oldman's Dracula aging and youthening between scenes, Kilar's score at full orchestral grief, effects done in-camera like a 1920s stage illusion. Hart's script keeps Stoker's plot skeleton and replaces its heart — the Count is no longer an invading contagion but a widower four centuries into mourning, and Mina his lost wife returned. **Verdict:** A magnificent, incoherent romance wearing a horror classic's name: faithful to the novel's events in sequence, faithless to its meaning in nearly every scene — and so committed to its own invention that the betrayal plays as grandeur.

## The contextual pivot
*Bram Stoker’s Dracula* opened in November 1992 as an act of career rescue dressed as a prestige production: Coppola, coming off *The Godfather Part III*’s mixed reception and in need of a commercial hit, took Columbia’s gothic property and shot it as an old master’s opera — almost entirely on soundstages, with effects achieved in camera, through matte paintings, miniatures, and shadow-play, a deliberate refusal of the digital age arriving around it. The possessive title was the marketing’s promise and the film’s lie: Hart’s script keeps Stoker’s plot skeleton but grafts on a reincarnation romance — the Count as a widower raging at God since 1462, Mina as his lost Elisabeta returned — imported from the Dan Curtis television tradition rather than the novel. It worked: the film was a hit, won three Academy Awards, and reset Dracula’s popular image for a generation — not Stoker’s invading contagion but the tragic lover in the red robe, an interpretation so successful that later adaptations now adapt Coppola as much as Stoker.

## Deep-dive critique
Ballhaus’s cinematography and the in-camera effects program are the film’s thesis in craft: dissolves that bleed scenes into each other, Dracula’s shadow moving against its owner, a train crossing a diary page, the whole film behaving like a haunted magic-lantern show — cinema remembering its own childhood while filming literature’s. Oldman’s performance is the center and the gamble: aged to a powdered ruin in the castle scenes, rejuvenated into a Byronic prince in London, speaking in an accent of his own invention, he plays grief, appetite, and blasphemy in rotation and somehow keeps them one creature — virtuosity that is also characterization, since this Dracula is literally a being of performances. Ryder’s Mina, given the desire and agency Stoker denied her, meets him halfway; Hopkins plays Van Helsing as a gleeful brute theologian, the film’s id in a frock coat. The famous failure is Reeves’s Harker — an accent and a bewilderment the film never believes in — and it matters because Harker is the audience’s road into the castle; the first act wobbles on him before Oldman takes the film over. Kilar’s score does the emotional labor the romance requires, full-orchestra mourning with a war drum under it. The costs are Stoker’s: the novel’s horror is invasion — a contagion with a deed to a London house, evil as logistics — and the film dissolves that cold, documentary dread into operatic sympathy, so the ending’s mercy-killing plays as a lovers’ pact rather than an extermination. Magnificent, incoherent, and entirely committed: the rare adaptation that betrays its source into a second classic.

## Adaptation ledger
- **Characterization of Dracula:** Oldman’s portrayal emphasizes tragic love over pure evil, altering the character's fundamental nature.
- **Mina’s Agency:** The film gives Mina a more proactive role, diverging from her passive depiction in the novel.
- **Love Triangle:** The focus on the relationship dynamics adds layers absent in the original text.
- **Visual Symbolism:** The film invents dramatic visuals, like the blood-infused scenes, to represent emotional turmoil.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument: whether the reincarnation romance is a key to the novel or a coup against it. Stoker's Dracula has no interiority and no grief; Coppola's has nothing else. The room divides on whether giving the monster a love story deepens the horror or dissolves it.
