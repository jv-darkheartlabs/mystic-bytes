## Hook & thesis
The expansions are itemizable: Azog promoted from a name in the appendices to a vendetta, Radagast on a rabbit sled, the White Council playing *Lord of the Rings* foreshadowing in a side room, and a barrel of action set-pieces the book narrates in a paragraph. Against all that stands the material Jackson shoots simply: Freeman's Bilbo, fussing and brave in exactly Tolkien's proportions, and the Gollum sequence — two actors and a riddle game — which needs nothing added and gets nothing added. **Verdict:** A prequel wearing its source as a subplot: the children's book about a hobbit who would rather be home keeps surfacing through the franchise machinery built on top of it, and every surfacing is the film at its best.

## The contextual pivot
*An Unexpected Journey* opened in December 2012 as the most overdetermined production in modern fantasy: a 300-page children’s book, published in 1937 as a bedtime story with maps, expanded into the first act of a trilogy meant to succeed the most successful adaptation trilogy ever made. The production’s history is a palimpsest of ambitions — del Toro attached for years and gone, Jackson returning reluctantly to a world he’d already completed, the two-film plan becoming three mid-production, and the whole enterprise shot at 48 frames per second, a technological wager that made Middle-earth look, to many eyes, like a television studio. The film arrived carrying its predecessor’s reputation as both asset and sentence: every frame measured against a trilogy it was only supposed to precede. What it documents, historically, is the moment franchise logic fully absorbed adaptation logic — the source no longer a story to be told but a territory to be held, its appendices mined, its margins promoted, its small hero’s journey reframed as the first movement of a war the book never asked him to witness.

## Deep-dive critique
Lesnie’s cinematography has the impossible task of making the same country look younger: New Zealand again, but glossier — digital sheen and the 48fps clarity stripping the grain that gave the earlier films their storybook distance, so that Bag End reads as a set tour and the trolls as very expensive television. Jackson’s direction is two films alternating. In the first, he trusts the book: the unexpected party played as comedy of manners, Freeman’s Bilbo fussing over doilies while his life is dismantled — a performance in exactly Tolkien’s proportions, politeness and buried courage in constant negotiation — and Riddles in the Dark, two actors, a cave, a game, the best sequence in the film precisely because nothing has been added to it. In the second film, everything is added: Azog’s vendetta bolted on as a personal engine, Radagast’s rabbit sled, goblin-town as a physics-defying ride, stone giants as spectacle without consequence — set-pieces the book narrates in a paragraph, inflated until Bilbo’s growth has to be announced in dialogue because the film has no quiet left to show it in. Armitage plays Thorin as an exiled king out of the later books, bombast where the novel keeps a pompous, brave, fallible dwarf; McKellen, given the White Council material, plays *Rings* foreshadowing in a side room like a man visiting his own sequel. Shore’s score quotes the earlier themes like heirlooms carried into a bigger house. The closing tease — an eye opening in a mountain of gold — is the franchise speaking, not the story. The children’s book keeps surfacing through the machinery, and every surfacing is the film at its best; the ratio between the two is the review.

## Adaptation ledger
- **Addition of the White Council:** The film introduces a subplot involving the White Council, adding layers of political intrigue absent in the book, but straying far from Tolkien’s original focus on Bilbo's journey.
- **Expanded character arcs:** Thorin's backstory is elaborated, shifting his portrayal from a noble leader to one burdened by greed, which alters the thematic core of courage and camaraderie in favor of darkness.
- **Invention of Azog the Defiler:** This new antagonist injects a sense of urgency and danger, yet feels like a distraction from the original conflict with Smaug, diluting the narrative's simplicity.
- **Altered pacing and structure:** The decision to elongate the tale sacrifices the story's natural rhythm, with moments of levity lost in the intricate plot expansion.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument: whether expansion is adaptation at all past a certain ratio. The room divides between those who welcome the appendices material as Tolkien too — the wider war the book only gestures at — and those who hold that a story's scale is part of its meaning, and tripling the canvas falsifies the tale of a small person.

**Questions for the room:**

- Riddles in the Dark works by subtraction. What does its success accuse the rest of the film of?
- Azog's vendetta gives Thorin a personal enemy the book never needed. What does a hero gain from a nemesis — and what does Bilbo's story lose?
- Freeman's Bilbo is pitched to the book while the film around him is pitched to *The Lord of the Rings*. Can both pitches be right?
- The 48fps experiment and the CGI sheen: does Middle-earth photograph better, or render better?
- Radagast and the Necromancer thread import a darkness Tolkien kept offstage. Is the offstage darkness scarier?
