## Hook & thesis
District 13 is shot as a bunker and a television studio at once, which is the film's whole argument: the rebellion needs Katniss the way the Capitol did, as footage. Her failed studio propos — a traumatized girl who cannot act, directed by committee — are the most honest scenes in the series, and 'The Hanging Tree,' sung cracked and plain, became a real protest song in the real world, which no one involved could have fully intended. Hutcherson, mostly on screens, plays Peeta's deterioration in interviews: weight, light, and wrongness accumulating. **Verdict:** A half-film that knows it: the source's first half is preparation and dread, and the adaptation honors that shape instead of inflating it — which makes it the series' most faithful entry and its least satisfying evening, both on purpose and by commerce.

## The contextual pivot
*Mockingjay – Part 1* arrived in November 2014 as the franchise’s most candidly commercial object: the final novel split in two, following the *Potter* and *Twilight* precedents, at the exact moment the series’ cultural authority peaked — *Catching Fire* had made the salute a real protest gesture in Thailand, where students were detained for flashing it. The split’s defenders and critics were both right about what it produced: a film with no arena, no games, and no climax, about the manufacture of propaganda — released into a media environment saturated with actual war footage and actual information campaigns, which made its subject uncomfortably current. It also carried a real loss inside its production: Philip Seymour Hoffman, its Plutarch, died during filming, and the film is dedicated to him; his remaining work was completed without digital resurrection, a restraint the industry would not always show afterward. Part 1 is the series’ greyest, slowest, most argued-over entry — a blockbuster about how revolutions are televised, made by the machinery it depicts.

## Deep-dive critique
Willems shoots District 13 in concrete and fluorescent grey, a bunker designed like a television studio — the rebellion’s aesthetic deliberately mirroring the Capitol’s, both regimes fluent in footage. Lawrence the director leans into the stasis: the film’s set-pieces are a failed studio shoot, a field trip to a bombed hospital, a song. The propo sequences are the series’ most self-aware: Katniss cannot act, the committee cannot direct authenticity, and the film’s argument about manufactured symbols is made by showing the manufacturing fail. Jennifer Lawrence plays trauma as depletion rather than display — the cracked, plain singing of ‘The Hanging Tree,’ which escaped the film into real charts and real protests, works because she undersells it, a girl reciting rather than performing. Hutcherson, confined to broadcast interviews, does remarkable work in degradation by increments — weight, light, and wrongness accumulating across three transmissions — while Moore’s Coin introduces the rebellion’s cold center in stillness, a president already practicing being Snow. Howard’s score keeps the motifs under concrete, surfacing mainly for the song’s orchestral afterlife. The costs are the split’s own: two hours of preparation ending on a rescue the film treats as a climax because the calendar says so; Gale remains a function in Hemsworth’s hands; and the raid’s cross-cutting with Snow’s phone call borrows tension from a confrontation Part 2 will have to stage again. It is the series’ most faithful film and its least complete one, and the fidelity and the incompleteness are the same decision.

## Adaptation ledger
- **Character Omissions:** Several supporting characters from the novel are omitted, diluting the novel’s rich tapestry of rebellion and resistance.
- **Dialogue Alterations:** Key dialogues that delve into Katniss’s emotional struggles are replaced with more politically charged speeches, which somewhat depersonalizes her journey.
- **Pacing Changes:** The film expands certain plot points, such as the propaganda campaign, while glossing over pivotal character arcs, impacting the emotional weight of the story.
- **Visual Storytelling:** The adaptation leans heavily on visual metaphors to convey thematic depth, a departure from the narrative exposition in the text.
- **Ending Shift:** The film’s conclusion is a stark cliffhanger, whereas the novel offers more closure, leaving the audience yearning for emotional resolution.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument: whether the split was a reading of the book or a reading of the market. The room divides between those who defend the breathing room — Collins' novel is about aftermath, and aftermath takes time — and those who count two hours of buildup ending on a rescue and call it an invoice.

**Questions for the room:**

- The propo scenes argue that authenticity can't be directed. Does the film apply that lesson to itself?
- Coin and Plutarch handle Katniss exactly as Snow handled his victors, with better catering. Does the film judge them, or just observe?
- Peeta exists as broadcast. What does the film gain by making the audience share Katniss's mediated access to him?
- "The Hanging Tree" escaped the film into actual protests. Does an adaptation get credit for its accidents?
- The rescue mission cross-cuts Snow's phone call with the raid — the film's one thriller sequence. Is its tension earned, or borrowed from Part 2?
