## Hook & thesis
Horton Foote's script trusts Lee's structure: a child's summer, a father's trial, a town indicted by its own courthouse. Peck's Atticus anchors it so completely that the performance replaced the character in the culture's memory. **Verdict:** Faithful, graceful, and genuinely moving — and, like the novel, a story about racism told through the white conscience it comforts, with Tom Robinson's inner life left where Lee left it: off the page, off the screen.

## The contextual pivot
*To Kill a Mockingbird* opened on Christmas Day 1962, into the middle of the civil rights movement it could only address in period dress: Lee’s novel, two years old and already a Pulitzer winner, told a 1930s Alabama story that 1962 audiences were meant to recognize as unfinished business. The production was a model of literary custody — Foote, a playwright of Southern rooms, adapting; Peck, whose own conscience was the film’s insurance, taking the role that would absorb his public identity entirely. The film’s historical significance is its unusual fate: it did not merely adapt the book, it became the book’s public face, the version through which generations who read Lee in school had already seen Maycomb — Peck’s face on the novel in the culture’s mind, the summation as a civic text. Later argument about both works — the white-conscience frame, Tom Robinson’s absence from his own story, and finally *Go Set a Watchman*’s complication of Atticus himself — has never dislodged the film, which remains the rare adaptation canonized alongside its source, each certifying the other.

## Deep-dive critique
Harlan shoots Maycomb in black and white that already feels like memory — soft Southern light, porches and courthouse square composed as a child’s geography, the street narrowing toward the Radley place like the end of the known world — and Mulligan directs from Scout’s height, the camera’s loyalties set at four feet: adults arrive as voices, legs, a father seen across rooms. Foote’s script trusts Lee’s structure absolutely, summer then trial then autumn, and the patience is the craft: the film earns its courtroom by first building the town that will sit in it. Peck’s Atticus is the performance that replaced a character in the culture’s memory — stillness as authority, the summation delivered not as oratory but as a man asking his neighbors to do arithmetic they already know — and the film is honest enough to let the verdict land as foregone: the jury was never the suspense. Badham’s Scout and Alford’s Jem carry the narration’s double register, children playing at the edge of an adult catastrophe; Duvall, in his film debut, makes Boo’s emergence a study in withheld presence finally spent in one doorway. Bernstein’s score, a child’s piano figure opening into strings, sets the memory key and never forces it. The costs are the source’s own, preserved by fidelity: Tom Robinson’s inner life left off-screen as Lee left it off the page, the story’s racism examined through the white conscience it comforts — a limitation the film shares with the novel and that later decades have named more plainly. Graceful, faithful, genuinely moving; and a period piece now twice over, about what 1935 did and what 1962 could say about it.

## Adaptation ledger
- Atticus Finch's moral complexity is preserved, showcasing his dedication to justice.  
- Scout's perspective is maintained, allowing viewers to witness events through her innocent eyes.  
- The trial scenes are dramatized for cinematic effect, heightening tension without sacrificing fidelity to the source.  
- Minor characters like Boo Radley are more visually present, showcasing their significance in Scout’s journey.  
- Some dialogue is adapted to enhance clarity while preserving the essence of Lee's prose.

## Discussion launchpad
The argument isn't whether the film is faithful — it's what its faithfulness preserves. The room divides on the frame itself: a child's-eye view that makes injustice legible to generations of readers, or a vantage that keeps the victims at the edge of their own story.
