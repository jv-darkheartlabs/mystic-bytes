I almost took a cheap room in a regional town because the photos looked like a postcard. The upload on the listing was a speedtest screenshot from years ago. Two client calls later I would have been paying for that discount in lost hours and a ruined Tuesday.

Cheap rent that drops calls is not cheap. It is expensive with a discount label. In Oceania the trap is scenic. A deck. A hill. A beach at the end of a road that fibre never quite reached. Auckland CBD and the inner suburbs cost more, and you can often buy a line that holds a meeting. An hour out, the view improves and the packet loss starts.

I start with connectivity, not charm. Coverage maps. A coworking day pass in the neighborhood before I sign anything. A cellular backup that actually works at the address, not at the café down the hill. Listings that say "great Wi-Fi" without a plan name are marketing. Fibre with a provider you can name is a spec.

Shoulder season helps. Late autumn and early spring in Aotearoa, the shoulder months in parts of Australia, you can get a room without peak-summer pricing and without competing with every other laptop for the same four cafés. You still pay for the line. Do not confuse a quieter street with a cheaper stack.

Compare total cost: rent, transit, mobile data, the days you will hot-desk because the house connection lied. A slightly higher rent with included fibre often beats a bargain that sends you out every morning. Smaller NZ cities and regional Australian towns can work if your work is async-first. They fail when you must be live on US East hours. Sleep debt becomes a line item. Auckland is pricey and the trains are not a personality. Dunedin, Wellington, a Gold Coast suburb off the strip: cheaper, sometimes, until you count the hours you spend proving the internet.

Affordable is a spreadsheet you update after a speedtest at 9am and 7pm. I left Louisiana knowing how to live on less. Aotearoa taught me that less is not worth it if the call dies while you are speaking.
