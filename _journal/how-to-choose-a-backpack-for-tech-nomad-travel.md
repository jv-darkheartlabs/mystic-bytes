I loaded the candidate bag with the real kit and walked a block. Shoulder, hip, sternum. If the demo unit in the shop is stuffed with tissue paper, you are buying a photograph. Fit is the first filter. Brand stories about founders and "adventure" are noise. A bag that wrecks your shoulder wrecks the sprint by day three.

Laptop access pattern comes next. Security lines and café tables want different things. I want a sleeve I can open without emptying the bag onto a tray, and I want the machine not bouncing against a bottle. Clamshell packing helps cubes stay honest. A top-only hole turns every adapter hunt into a roadside unpack. I have done that unpack on a Wellington footpath in the rain. Water resistance beats a promise of waterproof. Rain happens. Zippers fail. A rain cover in the lid is dull and it earns its grams.

Weight is a budget. Every extra pocket is a place to put something you did not need. I set a loaded ceiling I can lift into an overhead bin without performing. Hip belt if the load is over about 10kg and the walk is real. If you only ever roll a carry-on, the backpack is a personal item and the rules change: smaller, stricter, no "just one more charger." One-bag travel is a discipline, not a moral high ground. It forces gear honesty. Two bags can be the right call if the work kit is heavy and your back is not a slogan.

Limited-edition colors and packing cubes sold as identity are noise. A sternum strap that actually closes is not. Neither is a luggage pass-through if I am rolling a suitcase that week, or a bottle pocket that does not soak the laptop. I try the bag at the end of a workday, tired, because that is when you will wear it.

Louisiana humidity and Auckland drizzle ask for the same thing: a bag you can still pick up after the weather has had a turn. If you cannot lift it comfortably at home, you packed feelings. Unpack until the walk is boring. Boring is the design goal.

— JV · Dark Heart Labs.
