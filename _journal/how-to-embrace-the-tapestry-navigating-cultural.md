In New Orleans you talk while the line moves. You fill the air because silence can feel like a slight. In Tāmaki Makaurau I learned to shut up and wait, and for a month it felt like failing a test nobody had announced.

Cultural difference on the road is mostly manners. How long you hold eye contact. Whether you interrupt. Whether you photograph a ceremony because the light is good. None of that is a personality brand. It is the ordinary work of not making someone else's Tuesday about your curiosity.

I used to treat difference as material. A phrase overheard. A market stall. A greeting I could repeat later as proof I had been paying attention. That is collecting. The city becomes a mood board. People become texture. You can do this without meaning harm and still be rude.

Waiting is the first correction. Buses, counters, appointments that start when they start. Louisiana heat taught me patience of a different kind: you wait because the air is thick and hurrying does not help. Aotearoa taught me a quieter version. You wait because the person in front of you is not a delay in your itinerary.

Listening is the second. Ask one question. Let the answer finish. Do not rush to map it onto a story you already had about how they do things here. If you do not understand a custom, you can say so without turning the moment into content. You do not owe a caption to a day that was not yours to narrate.

Manners are specific. Shoes off. Voice down in shared hallways. No filming at a marae unless you were invited and told where to stand. Learn the local word for please even if your accent is a mess. Effort without performance looks like homework, not a reel.

I still get this wrong. I still fill silence when I am nervous. The point is not fluency in every room. The point is refusing to harvest a place for proof that you were open-minded. Stay long enough to be corrected. Then take the correction without turning it into a lesson you teach strangers on the internet.
