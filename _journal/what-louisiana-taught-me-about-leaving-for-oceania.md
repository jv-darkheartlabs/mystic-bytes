August in New Orleans teaches you that weather is a schedule. You do not fight the humidity. You dress for it, you move slower before noon, and you assume the afternoon will ask more of your body than a calendar slot admits. I left carrying that weather memory the way other people carry a coat. Aotearoa does not owe me the same heat. It owes me a different honesty: wet wind, short winter light, a supermarket that closes while a Louisiana brain is still planning a late errand.

Bureaucracy at home often ran on who you knew. A form, a cousin, a parish office that softened if you showed up in person and spoke like a neighbour. That skill is real. It is also a habit to unlearn at a border that wants documents, not charm. Tāmaki Makaurau queues are not rude. They are literal. I keep copies now because Louisiana taught me that paper vanishes, and because Oceania taught me that "I can get it later" is not a plan.

Community speed is the third lesson. New Orleans will pull you into a conversation you did not schedule. That generosity built a mouth that can talk to strangers without treating them like networking. Keep that. Drop the assumption that loud rooms equal progress. Aotearoa can be reserved in ways that read as cold if you only know the Gulf. Directness here is often kindness. Filling silence with performance is not.

What I kept: feeding myself well on a budget. Rice, beans, a market bird, fruit that is actually ripe. Fabrics that forgive sweat. The instinct to check on people after weather. What I unlearned: 24-hour everything as a birthright. The idea that every city has a pharmacy and a meal waiting at 1am. The reflex to treat a new place as a stage.

When I compare cities I do not rank them. I list the daily tradeoffs. Heat versus drizzle. Kinship versus paperwork. A hello that takes twenty minutes versus a nod that still means you can share a footpath. Leaving for Oceania did not erase Louisiana. It made the keep-pile obvious. The rest I folded, thanked, and did not put in the bag.
