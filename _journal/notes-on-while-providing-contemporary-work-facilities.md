A loft with good light and a bad acoustic is a photoshoot. A workplace has hours you can trust and a screen that is not a laptop hinge. I visit at the hour I will actually sit, not at 10am when the marketing photos were taken. Peak noise tells the truth. So does the 6pm emptying, if that is when my US calls begin.

I buy a day pass before a month. I sit where members sit, not in the window they use for Instagram. I ask whether the monitors exist, whether they are booked by 9, and whether the standing desks are theatre. Phone booths that are always full are a data point. A Slack no one reads is another. Community on the website is not culture on the floor.

Member culture is the quiet test. Do people take calls at the hot desks, or do they walk to a room? Do they leave crumbs for the closer? Can you be a regular without joining a personality cult about remote life? I have left beautiful spaces in Auckland because the espresso machine sat next to the only quiet table and nobody thought that was a problem. I have stayed in plainer rooms because the door to the call booth closed and the hours matched the work.

This is not the portable hotel-desk note. That kit is a stand, a mouse, a pouch, and a bedspread you refuse to work from. Coworking is a building with other humans. I need it when the short-stay Wi-Fi is a toy and the chair is a dining stool. I do not need it when it costs a week of groceries to sit among people performing busyness.

Louisiana offices taught me that fluorescent honesty beats a brick wall that echoes. Aotearoa winters taught me to ask about heating and whether the space opens on the public holiday I forgot existed. I write the constraints down: noise I can survive, hours that cover the overlap, a monitor I can plug into, a culture that treats work as work. If the loft fails two of those, I keep walking. The photograph can find someone else.

— JV · Dark Heart Labs.
