The landlord's speed test at 11 a.m. is theater. The building is empty, the listing is live, and everyone wants the number to look kind. I run mine at dinner, when the apartments fill up and the neighborhood starts streaming. If I cannot be in the unit at peak, I come back, or I do not sign.

Download is the vanity metric. Upload is what a video call actually spends. I test both, more than once, on the network I would use for work, not on a phone hotspot I brought as a prop. Ping toward the region my teammates live in if I can. A pretty local speed that dies on the path to a US Git host is still a failed office.

Then I make a real call. Camera on, screen share, the tools I would use in a review. Speed-test pages do not stutter the way a standup does. If the picture tiles or the audio falls off, I have my answer. I would rather look slightly rude in a showing than pay a deposit on a pipe that cannot hold a conversation.

Cellular failover is part of the lease decision, not a later hobby. I check whether my SIM or eSIM gets usable data in that room, not on the sidewalk. Concrete, hills, and old wiring in Tāmaki Makaurau have surprised me. A backup that only works downstairs is not a backup. I also want a coworking option I would actually use if the fiber dies for a week, costed as insurance, not as a lifestyle add-on.

Listing adjectives do not count. "Fibre" and "high-speed" are marketing. Ask the ISP name, whether the line is shared, and what happens when it breaks. A host who cannot answer is telling you something.

The 72-hour base protocol is about surviving arrival. This is about not signing. Walk if the peak test fails. A cheaper unit with a proven pipe beats a pretty view that drops every evening. I can live with a dull kitchen. I cannot ship from a rumor of broadband.
