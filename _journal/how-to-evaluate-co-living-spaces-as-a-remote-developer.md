The house Slack had a pinned message about dishes that ran longer than some API docs I have shipped. That was the tell. Co-living is a lease on people. The house rules are the contract. If the contract is vague, you will debug other people's moods at 2am.

I read listings the way I read an OpenAPI spec. Quiet hours: written down, or "we are chill" which means someone will take a call from the kitchen during yours. Cleaning: a rota with names, or a feeling that someone else will do it. Guest policy: overnight visitors allowed with notice, or a surprise sofa guest on the night you have a review. Upload speed: a number you can verify, or marketing Wi-Fi with a pretty courtyard.

Ask how many residents run video calls at once. One fibre line serving eight stand-ups is a congestion problem dressed as community. Ask about desk space that is not a dining table. Ask whether the house has a room you can close. If the answer is "we work from cafés," you are paying rent for a social club with bunk beds.

Visit at night if you can. Noise travels after 9. Music from a hallway. A door that slams. The person who cooks with every pan. Daytime tours lie the way demo environments lie. Read the house chat if they offer a preview. Tone leaks there first: passive-aggressive cleaning notes, or adults who can name a problem without a paragraph of therapy.

Good co-living reduces the loneliness that shows up when you change cities. Bad co-living adds meeting fatigue you did not put on the calendar. You will spend more energy on shared-fridge politics than on the work that pays the rent.

I want a two-week trial before a long stay. If they will not do it, I treat that as a 403. In Tāmaki Makaurau the better houses were the boring ones: posted hours, a labelled shelf, a measured upload, nobody performing "intentional community" in the group chat. I will take a lease that behaves like a contract over a family that wants me to be grateful.

— JV · Dark Heart Labs.
