I stopped joining groups named after a lifestyle. The rooms that helped were the ones where someone argued about a pull request and then went quiet so a person on a call could finish a sentence.

If you want other builders, go where building happens. A coworking day pass you actually use twice. A local meetup with a talk, not a rooftop mixer. An open-source sprint with a ticket board and a clock. Introduce yourself with what you ship, or what you are stuck on. Geography is context. It is not a personality.

Generic Facebook groups for people who "work from anywhere" taught me nothing I could use on a Tuesday. The feed was visas as folklore, coworking as content, and a lot of strangers performing ease. I needed overlap: similar craft, hours that can meet, and respect for focus. That overlap showed up in smaller rooms. A Rails meetup in Wellington. A weekend hack day. The Slack for a project I already contributed to, where someone said they were in Auckland this month.

Oceania tech communities are smaller than the ones I knew from US cities. That is an advantage if you show up twice. People remember the person who fixed a projector, who reviewed a README, who did not treat the after-event pint as a networking circuit. Offer help before you ask for introductions. Debugging for a local who will later tell you which ISP to avoid is a better trade than collecting faces.

Schedule matters as much as stack. A builder on US hours and a builder on NZST can share a city and never share a lunch. Say your working window out loud. Find the people whose quiet hours look like yours. Mutual respect for focus is the filter. If the group chats through every call, you have found company, not colleagues.

I am not looking for a scene. I am looking for two or three people who understand why I will leave a café when the playlist gets ambitious. Louisiana taught me that work friends are the ones who let you finish. Aotearoa is the same, with better coffee and a smaller room.

— JV · Dark Heart Labs.
