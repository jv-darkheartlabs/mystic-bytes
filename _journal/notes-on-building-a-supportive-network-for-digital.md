Professional contacts are not the people who notice if you go quiet for ten days. I keep two lists, and I do not mix them. Home people: family in Louisiana, friends who knew me before the hemisphere changed. Local people: the ones who can meet for coffee in Tāmaki Makaurau, who know which pharmacy is open late, who will answer a text when the apartment Wi-Fi dies on a Sunday.

Distance eats the first list if you wait for a feeling. I put check-ins on a cadence that is boring on purpose. A weekly message to my mother that is not a performance of my itinerary. A fortnightly call with a friend who does not need a travel report. Voice when I can. Text when the timezone is ugly. The point is that they hear from me before they have to wonder.

Local people are built slower. I do not collect them at events and call it a network. I return to the same café. I learn a name. I say yes to a walk that is not drinks with the industry. If I am only in a city for two weeks, I am honest about that. A supportive local is allowed to be a barista who remembers the order. That still counts.

I tell home people when I am moving, not after I have landed and gone silent for a week. Silence reads as trouble. A short note — new address, new hours, I am fine — beats a long essay after the fact. I also tell them what I need: a signature on a form, a package held, a conversation that is not about visas.

This is not professional networking. I do not ask these people for intros to hiring managers. I ask them to remain in my life. The work will have its own channels. Support is the human backup when the work is fine and the day is not.

If the cadence slips, I restart it. Guilt is useless. A message on Thursday still arrives.
