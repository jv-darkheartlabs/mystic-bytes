## Hook & thesis
Lewis insisted Narnia was not allegory but *supposal* — suppose there were a world like Narnia, and the Son took shape there as a Lion — and the distinction matters, because the book works on children who know nothing of the theology. What they remember is the Turkish Delight, the betrayal, the Stone Table cracking, spring arriving as a rumor of running water. Edmund is the brave choice: not a villain but a sulky, bullied, ordinary boy whose treachery is bought with sweets and spite — and whose rescue is what both the Deep Magic and the Deeper Magic turn out to be about. **Verdict:** The founding text of portal fantasy and still the most reread. Its costs are the ones Lewis accepted openly: the moral architecture is load-bearing Christianity, the girls’ battle exemption is of its 1950 moment, and Father Christmas arming the children is glorious or alarming depending on the reader’s age.

## The contextual pivot
*The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* was published in 1950, the first of the seven Narnia books Lewis wrote in a burst across the early fifties. It began, Lewis said, with an image carried since he was sixteen: a faun with an umbrella and parcels in a snowy wood, which waited thirty years for the evacuated children of the war — Lewis took evacuees in at the Kilns — to walk into it. Tolkien, his closest reader, disliked it: the mixture of mythologies offended his sense of consistency, Father Christmas beside dryads beside a witch out of Andersen. Posterity sided with the children. The book founded portal fantasy as a popular form and made Lewis — a don known for scholarship and Christian apologetics — into a children’s author for the ages. The argument over whether its theology is the story’s engine or its stowaway has run continuously ever since, which Lewis, who insisted on ‘supposal, not allegory,’ would have counted as the book working as intended.

## Deep-dive critique
The narration is third-person omniscient with a storyteller’s license: Lewis’s narrator confides in the reader, comments on the children, and moves between heads and scenes as an uncle would — by association rather than by rule. The prose is famously plain, Lewis having distrusted ornament in children’s books, and the plainness is a delivery system: sentences of near-biblical simplicity carry the Stone Table sequence, where the style rises to liturgy without changing its vocabulary. The pacing is brisk to the point of abruptness: a reign of fifteen years takes a paragraph, battles happen offstage or in summary, and the Witch — the book’s most efficient creation, tyranny as eternal winter and a police force of statues — is dispatched by Aslan within a page. Characterization is typological by design: the children are dispositions — the just eldest, the traitor, the believer, the valiant — more than psychologies, with Edmund alone granted an interior: spite, shame, and the slow education of being forgiven, which is why his arc carries the whole theology. The famous mixture Tolkien deplored — Father Christmas, fauns, Norse and classical and Christian figures sharing one wood — is either incoherence or the book’s actual claim: that all the old stories rhyme. Readers have been choosing sides for seventy years, and the choosing is the afterlife.

## Discussion launchpad
Lewis’s own distinction is the argument: a story that *supposes* rather than encodes. Whether the difference survives contact with the Stone Table is the discussion.

**Questions for the room:**  
- Does the supposal/allegory distinction hold up in the reading — and does it change what the book is allowed to do?  
- Edmund’s is the most ordinary treason in children’s literature — sweets and wounded pride. Why does the book stake its theology on its least likable child?  
- *Always winter and never Christmas* may be the most efficient description of tyranny ever written for children. What does the Witch’s rule actually consist of — statues, informers, an endless season?  
- Aslan’s death is negotiated in private, on terms revealed only afterward. Ransom, trick, or love — which transaction does the Deeper Magic describe?  
- You met this again at Grade 11, between Gatsby and The Hobbit. What does a childhood book do differently at sixteen — and which shelf does it belong on now?
