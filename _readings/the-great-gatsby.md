## Hook & thesis
Gatsby is a haunting narrated by a bystander. Nick Carraway takes the cottage next door, reserves judgment he doesn’t actually withhold, and assembles — out of parties, rumors, and one long confession over tea — the shape of a man who built a mansion and a criminal fortune as a delivery mechanism for a single moment: Louisville, 1917, before the war, when Daisy Fay’s voice was “full of money” and Jay Gatz’s self-invention was still brand new. *Can’t repeat the past? Why of course you can!* is the novel’s most honest and most damned sentence. Against it stands Nick’s final verdict on the Buchanans — careless people who smash things and retreat into their money and let others clean up. **Verdict:** The best-constructed American novel of its century, sentence for sentence. Its cost is Daisy, given a voice full of money and almost no interiority — the book’s subject and its blind spot in one person.

## The contextual pivot
*The Great Gatsby* was published in April 1925 and was, by Fitzgerald’s own ledger, a disappointment — decent reviews, modest sales, nothing like the success of *This Side of Paradise* five years earlier. When he died in 1940, he believed it had been forgotten. Its resurrection is the famous accident of its afterlife: the Armed Services Edition, some 155,000 copies handed to soldiers in the Second World War, seeded a generation of readers, and the postwar academy did the rest — installing it as the Great American Novel and the default high-school text on the Dream. The Jazz Age romp its first readers took it for is still visible inside the canonical one: a book about bootleggers and parties, written by a man who was both, watching his own set with a participant’s knowledge and a hangover’s clarity. The copyright expired in 2021, and the sequels, prequels, and retellings have flooded in since — the culture now treating *Gatsby* the way Gatsby treated Daisy: as a past it insists on repeating.

## Deep-dive critique
The narration is first person, Nick’s — but Nick is a novelist inside the novel, writing two years later, arranging his material, and Fitzgerald exploits the double position ruthlessly: Nick is ‘within and without,’ participant and prosecutor, and his famous reserve of judgment is a style rather than a fact, since no narrator in American fiction judges more continuously. The prose is the book’s true event: sentences of such finish that the plot — thin, melodramatic, borrowed from the crime papers — is carried by rhythm the way the parties are carried by music. The construction is symphonic rather than linear: scenes recur with variation (two parties, two lunches, two drives across the ash heaps), images accrue — the green light, the eyes of Eckleburg, the valley of ashes as the book’s true center — and the Plaza confrontation is staged as the hinge everything before it was tuning. The flaws are the standing ones: Daisy is a voice and a symbol, granted no scene of solitary thought in the book that bears her gravity; Myrtle and Wilson are class instruments more than people; and the ending’s machinery — a mistaken car, a pool, a revolver — is pulp plotting that the prose has to lift, and mostly does. What no flaw touches is the compression: under fifty thousand words, and a whole civilization’s self-image fits inside it, with room left over for the boats against the current.

## Discussion launchpad
The syllabus calls it the American Dream novel. The book itself is more specific and less kind: a machine for sorting dreams by the age of the money behind them.

**Questions for the room:**  
- Is Gatsby’s project romantic, delusional — or the most American sentence in the book?  
- Nick calls himself one of the few honest people he’s known, and narrates a book full of his own omissions. What is he protecting — Gatsby, or himself?  
- Daisy: subject, or blind spot?  
- Tom and Daisy retreat into their money and let others clean up the mess. A century on, is the carelessness the most contemporary thing in the novel?  
- The green light gets assigned to every schoolchild in America. What did you find in this book at Grade 11 that the syllabus didn’t mention?
