## Hook & thesis
Everyone misremembers this book as a story about a monster. It’s a story about a parent. Victor Frankenstein’s crime isn’t the making; it’s the leaving — he animates a being and flees the room, and everything after is that being educating himself in a world that answers his face with horror. Shelley nests the tale in frames — Walton’s letters around Victor’s confession around the creature’s own narrative — and gives the “monster” the book’s most eloquent chapters: a mind that teaches itself language through a cottage wall and reads Paradise Lost as autobiography. **Verdict:** The founding text of science fiction, and still its most searching; its cost is Victor, whose self-pity the narration never indicts quite as hard as his creature does.

## The contextual pivot
*Frankenstein* began as a ghost-story contest in the Year Without a Summer — 1816, when ash from Tambora’s eruption dimmed the skies over Lake Geneva and the company at the Villa Diodati, Byron and Percy Shelley among them, amused a rained-in holiday by competing to frighten each other. Mary Godwin, eighteen, produced the seed of this novel, expanded it over the following year, and saw it published anonymously in 1818 — with a preface by Percy that led many readers to assume he had written it. Its science was the era’s live speculation: galvanism, Erasmus Darwin’s experiments, Humphry Davy’s chemistry. Its philosophy was the Godwin–Wollstonecraft inheritance: the creature is a mind made by treatment, not born evil. The 1831 revised edition, with Shelley’s famous introduction telling the origin story, softened the book’s radical edges and is the text most readers know. Between the two editions sits the founding document of science fiction — and the source of a myth that escaped its book entirely: the flat head, the bolts, the grunt, none of which are hers.

## Deep-dive critique
The structure is a set of nested confessions: Walton’s letters frame Victor’s narration, which frames the creature’s — and the creature’s, at the center, is the rhetorical summit of the book, an autodidact’s memoir learned through a cottage wall, *Paradise Lost* read as true history by a mind that cannot decide whether it is Adam or Satan. Shelley’s prose runs at two temperatures: Victor’s feverish Romantic rhetoric, all self-dramatizing weather, against the creature’s lucid, periodic sentences. The eloquence is the argument, since the novel’s whole case is that the ‘monster’ is the best mind in it. The craft flaws are a young novelist’s: the supporting cast — Elizabeth, Clerval, Justine — are virtues rather than people; the plot runs on withheld information, and Victor’s silence at Justine’s trial is the book’s most necessary and least believable hinge; the Arctic frame wraps the confession in an expedition for symmetry more than need. But the doubling is exact and deliberate: creator and creature mirror each other until the final chase reads as one man pursuing his own refusal across the ice. Every science-fiction story about a made mind since is written in this one’s margin.

## Discussion launchpad
The chain of catastrophe in this novel is not made of lightning or hubris. It’s made of refusals — a creator who won’t stay, a family that won’t look, a friend who won’t speak.

**Questions for the room:**  
- The creature asks for exactly one thing: a companion. Victor begins her, then destroys her. His most moral act, or his most cowardly — and what does the creature owe the world afterward?  
- Why does Shelley give the monster the best prose — and what does his eloquence do to the humans’ case?  
- Victor’s silence lets Justine hang. Itemize his silences: when does his suffering stop being tragedy and become negligence?  
- Walton turns his ship around — the only man who hears the confession and changes course. Why frame the whole book inside a near-repeat of Victor’s ambition?  
- Written by an eighteen-year-old, Wollstonecraft’s daughter, in the Year Without a Summer. Where do you see the biography in the book — and where does the book escape it?
