## Hook & thesis
Dracula is the rare monster who never tells his own story. Stoker builds the novel entirely out of other people’s documents — Harker’s journal, Mina’s letters, Seward’s phonograph diary, a ship’s log, newspaper cuttings — so the Count exists only as evidence: sightings, wounds, absences. The form *is* the horror. These are modern people armed with typewriters, shorthand, telegraphs and train timetables, collating themselves toward a conclusion none of them wants to reach — and the collator is Mina, who types the men’s scattered accounts into the single archive that becomes the weapon. **Verdict:** The founding text of the modern vampire, and still the strangest in structure; its cost is the back half’s chase, which runs on timetables and repetition once the archive has done its best work.

## The contextual pivot
*Dracula* was published in 1897, the year of Victoria’s Diamond Jubilee, and it’s saturated in its moment’s technologies and anxieties: typewriters, phonograph diaries, Kodak cameras, blood transfusion still new and half-understood, telegraphs and railway timetables deployed against a feudal aristocrat traveling by soil and ship. Stoker — theatre manager to Henry Irving, writing at night — built his Count from folklore research and travel books about a Transylvania he never visited, and from the era’s live fears: invasion from the empire’s edges, degeneration, the New Woman his heroines both are and aren’t. It sold steadily rather than spectacularly; the conquest came through stage and film, which fixed the cape-and-accent image the novel itself never describes. *Frankenstein*, so often shelved beside it as a contemporary, is older by nearly eighty years. *Dracula*’s true contemporaries are *Jekyll and Hyde* and *Dorian Gray* — the 1890s Gothic of doubled selves and imported corruption.

## Deep-dive critique
The epistolary structure is not a container but the argument: there is no narrator, only an archive, and the archive has an editor — Mina, whose typewriter collates the men’s scattered records into the dossier that makes the hunt possible. Stoker’s boldest choice is the Count’s silence: Dracula speaks only as reported speech, early, inside Harker’s journal; after that he exists as evidence — a smell, a wound, an empty box — which keeps him unknowable in a way no first-person monster can be. The multiple voices let Stoker modulate: Harker’s solicitor’s precision curdling into dread, Seward’s clinical phonograph diary, Lucy’s letters all brightness before the fall. The craft costs are in the back half. Once the archive is assembled, the novel becomes logistics — telegrams, train schedules, the long repetitive chase — and the Transylvanian climax is over in paragraphs, the Count dispatched almost offstage, as if Stoker’s engine, evidence, couldn’t power an ending built on confrontation. Quincey Morris, the American, exists largely to die patriotically. But the middle — Lucy’s sleepwalking, a staking performed as surgery and sacrament, Mina’s scar — is as controlled as horror gets: dread administered by document, each entry dated, each horror filed.

## Discussion launchpad
No chapter belongs to Dracula. Everything known about him is testimony — which makes this a novel about how a group of people agree on an impossible truth, and what the agreeing costs the women in the room.

**Questions for the room:**  
- What does the novel gain by keeping Dracula as pure evidence — and what would one chapter in his voice destroy?  
- Mina assembles the archive that hunts him. Is she the book’s true protagonist, disguised as its secretary?  
- Lucy is punished by the plot in proportion to her desire. Is the novel policing her, or exhibiting the policing?  
- It takes a committee — doctor, lawyer, lord, cowboy, professor — to kill one aristocrat. What does the team represent that a single hero couldn’t?  
- This is a Grade 11 required-reading staple. What does the novel hide from a classroom reading — and what does it smuggle in?
