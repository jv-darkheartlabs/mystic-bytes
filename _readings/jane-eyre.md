## Hook & thesis
Jane Eyre’s radicalism is bookkeeping. She audits every offer life makes her — Lowood’s charity, Rochester’s passion, St. John’s mission — against a single ledger: what it would cost her soul to accept. She is poor, plain, and entirely unwilling to be diminished, and Brontë lets her argue her case at a length and voltage almost no heroine of 1847 was permitted. **Verdict:** A novel about a woman who will not be made less, written at full charge; its cost is Bertha, spent as machinery so that Jane can be free.

## The contextual pivot
*Jane Eyre* was published in 1847 under a man’s name — Currer Bell — because Charlotte Brontë knew what would happen to a novel this angry if a woman signed it. It arrived the same season as *Wuthering Heights*, into a decade when the governess had become the Victorian era’s favorite problem: educated, genteel, and unemployable at any wage that preserved the gentility. Brontë took the Gothic’s furniture — the manor, the madwoman, the brooding master — and wired it to a class grievance. Its descendants split in two directions: the romance line, running through du Maurier’s *Rebecca* and every brooding-housekeeper plot since; and the answering line, through *Wide Sargasso Sea*’s reply from the attic and a century of heroines who negotiate instead of swoon. Few novels get to found two traditions. Fewer still get argued about, continuously, for 175 years.

## Deep-dive critique
The narration is the first craft decision, and everything else hangs on it: Jane tells her own story retrospectively, in the first person, with direct addresses — *Reader, I married him* is only the most quoted — that make the reader her confidant and her jury. The effect is advocacy. We get every event at the temperature Jane felt it, her own misjudgments left visible rather than edited out — the jealousy of Blanche Ingram, the credulity, the fury — which is why the voice convinces. Structurally the book is five houses and five Janes: Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield, Moor House, Ferndean, each with its own regime of power to refuse — the Reeds’ cruelty, Brocklehurst’s charity, Rochester’s passion, St. John’s purpose. The pacing sags where the machinery shows: the Madeira inheritance arriving by coincidence, and Rochester’s voice carried to Jane across a hundred miles of England — a supernatural touch the novel neither explains nor apologizes for. Gothic license, openly taken.

The flaw the book can’t argue its way out of is Bertha. As construction she is perfect — the laugh in the corridor, the torn veil, the fire — and as a person she is nothing: no speech, no history the novel trusts, a Creole heiress converted into an obstacle and then into the fire that clears the plot. Rochester’s self-pitying account of her is the only one we get, and Jane, who interrogates every other authority in the book, never interrogates his. The ending’s famous balance — her money, his maiming — is Brontë solving by arithmetic a problem the novel created by silence. That the book survives its own machinery is the measure of Jane’s voice: it is the one interior the novel never rations.

## Discussion launchpad
The ending is a negotiation, not a rescue: Jane returns with an inheritance of her own, Rochester returns blinded and maimed, and the book balances its marriage by force of plot. Whether that arithmetic is justice or bookkeeping is the argument the novel has been having with its readers for 175 years.

**Questions for the room:**  
- Jane refuses Rochester’s arrangement and St. John’s proposal alike. What exactly is she refusing in each case — and is it the same thing?  
- The marriage is balanced by giving Jane money and taking Rochester’s sight and a hand. Equality, or accounting?  
- Bertha narrates nothing. What does the novel gain from her silence — and what does it owe her?  
- Why does Brontë let Jane argue — “I am no bird” — at a length almost no heroine of her decade was allowed?  
- Do the Lowood chapters still bite as an indictment of charitable institutions, or have they become period furniture?
