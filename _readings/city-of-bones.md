## Hook & thesis
City of Bones runs on revelation. Clary Fray’s ordinary life — Brooklyn, her artist mother, her best friend Simon — is peeled back layer by layer: the Sight her mother hid from her, the father she never knew, a heritage other people wear written in runes on their skin. Clare’s New York is a palimpsest: demons in the club, angels in the bloodline, a warrior order hidden inside a cathedral. The engine underneath is inheritance — everything Clary learns is something her family kept from her. **Verdict:** Pure propulsion, and a world built to be lived in for six books; its cost is exposition — the lore arrives in speeches, and the ending’s great revelation is one the series itself would spend books renegotiating.

## The contextual pivot
*City of Bones* was published in 2007, at the height of the post-Potter, mid-*Twilight* YA surge, and Clare arrived with a biography that was itself a sign of the times: she had been a major figure in *Harry Potter* fandom — her Draco Trilogy was among the most-read fan fiction of its era — and *City of Bones* carries fandom’s DNA openly: the ensemble banter, the shipping architecture, the world built to be inhabited. Its New York of glamoured clubs and a cathedral-hidden Institute joined the urban-fantasy wave *Buffy* had started on television, but pitched at teenagers and organized around bloodline rather than the lone slayer. The series became a pillar of the Shadowhunter empire — six books in this sequence, multiple sequel series, a film, a television adaptation — and a clear demonstration that in 2000s YA, the world was the franchise.

## Deep-dive critique
The narration is third-person limited, held close to Clary — with occasional chapters handed to Jace, Simon, and others — a choice that matters because the book’s engine is withheld knowledge: a limited third lets Clare show us the shadow world at Clary’s shoulder while keeping Jocelyn’s secrets, and Valentine’s, offstage until their entrances. The prose is the era’s YA vernacular at its most fluent — simile-driven, banter-forward, Jace’s dialogue doing double duty as characterization and comic relief — and the pacing is propulsive to a fault: the middle is a relay of revelation scenes, each new location (the Institute, the City of Bones itself, Renwick’s) arriving with a lore briefing attached. The set pieces are genuinely strong — the Pandemonium opening, the Hotel Dumort rescue — but the climax spends its energy on a parentage revelation the series would later reverse, which retroactively taxes the whole book: a twist undone is a debt called in. What holds is the palimpsest city and the family machinery. Clare’s real subject, here as everywhere after, is inheritance — children paying interest on their parents’ wars.

## Discussion launchpad
Every secret in this book is a family secret. The demons are almost a distraction from the real haunting: what your parents were, and what they decided you didn’t need to know.

**Questions for the room:**  
- Jocelyn hid the entire shadow world from Clary, for her protection. Does the book vindicate that secrecy or convict it?  
- The Shadowhunters are a hereditary warrior caste with a supremacist streak. Does this novel notice its own heroes’ politics — or is that the sequels’ problem?  
- Simon is priced as baggage by the world he’s carried into. What does his presence let the book say that Clary’s story can’t?  
- The revelation about Clary and Jace: shock for its own sake, or a real move about identities handed down without consent?  
- Valentine is a true believer with a genocide in his past and a genuine point about the Clave’s corruption. Where does the book let him be right?
