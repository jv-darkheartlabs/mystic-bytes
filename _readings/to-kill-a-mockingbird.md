## Hook & thesis
Mockingbird is two books sharing one spine: a childhood idyll of summers, dares, and a phantom neighbor — and a trial that teaches the children what their town actually is. Lee’s structural gamble is that the Boo Radley plot, gothic and playful and resolved by a sheriff’s merciful lie, is the lens for the Tom Robinson plot, where no rescue comes and the lie the town tells is the verdict. Atticus has grown so monumental that the novel’s own complications get paved over: he is a man of his place and time as well as its conscience, conducting an honorable defense inside a system he does not try to break, while the Black community watches from the balcony — grateful, and given almost no interiority of its own. **Verdict:** The book America assigns its children to teach them empathy, and it works — which is its achievement and its limit. Its cost: the empathy flows toward the white family learning the lesson, while Tom Robinson, the lesson’s subject, gets a trial, a death, and very little self.

## The contextual pivot
*To Kill a Mockingbird* was published in July 1960 — months into the sit-in movement, the civil rights era arriving in real time around it — and won the Pulitzer Prize the following year. Lee, from Monroeville, Alabama, had been given a famous gift by friends in 1956: a year off from her airline-reservations job to write. The book drew on her own childhood street — her father a lawyer who had once defended Black men accused of murder, Truman Capote the summer neighbor who became Dill — and on the Scottsboro trials’ long shadow over Alabama justice. It became the most assigned novel in American schools and one of the most challenged, a dual citizenship it has held for sixty years. Lee published nothing else through a long silence, becoming American literature’s great recluse-author — until *Go Set a Watchman*, an early draft marketed in 2015 as a lost sequel, revealed a segregationist Atticus and turned her final year into an argument about consent, legacy, and who owns a reputation. The novel outlasts the argument, as it has outlasted every previous one.

## Deep-dive critique
The narration is first person, adult Scout’s, remembering child Scout — a double voice Lee manages with unusual honesty: the child’s perceptions are rendered at child scale (the Radley house a gothic universe, the trial first a spectacle and then a wound), while the adult’s sentences supply the irony and the mercy, and the seams between the two are where the novel’s intelligence lives. The prose is plain and funny — Maycomb rendered in a child’s taxonomy of families and a native’s ear for its talk. The structure is two plots geared together: the Radley plot teaches the children, and the reader, the book’s method — empathy as the work of imagining an interior — so the Robinson plot can test the method at scale and watch the town fail it. Atticus’s courtroom performance is built like a sermon and lands like one, which is both the book’s power and the standing critique: the Black community is positioned as audience — in the balcony, standing as he passes — witnesses to a drama of white conscience in which Tom Robinson himself is nearly silent, given dignity but almost no interiority, and killed offstage by seventeen bullets the novel reports rather than inhabits. The ending answers with its own moral complexity: Heck Tate’s lie, Boo carried home, Scout standing on the Radley porch seeing her street from inside another’s skin — the method, finally, performed perfectly, on the safe subject. Whether that ending is the book’s triumph or its evasion is the argument sixty years of readers have been having, and the book is large enough to sustain both verdicts.

## Discussion launchpad
The balcony is where the book puts its Black characters: above the action, watching, grateful. Sixty years of argument about this novel starts from those seats.

**Questions for the room:**  
- What does it do to the book that Tom Robinson gets almost no inner life while Boo Radley gets the ending?  
- Atticus defends honorably inside a system he doesn’t try to break. Conscience — or complicity with better manners?  
- Heck Tate’s closing lie — *Bob Ewell fell on his knife* — is the book’s one act of mercy, told by law enforcement. How does it sit beside a trial where the truth lost?  
- *Go Set a Watchman* showed an older, segregationist Atticus in a draft Lee never published as a sequel, and readers felt betrayed. Does it change Mockingbird? Should it?  
- This book is now assigned and banned, sometimes in the same state. What is each side afraid it teaches?
